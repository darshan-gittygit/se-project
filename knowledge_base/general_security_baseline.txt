General Security and Privacy Baseline (Domain-Agnostic)

Regardless of industry, most modern software systems that handle user accounts,
personal data, or business-critical operations are expected to meet a baseline
set of security and privacy practices, independent of any specific regulatory
framework.

This baseline typically includes: authenticating users before granting access to
their data or account (password, multi-factor authentication, or equivalent),
encrypting sensitive data both in transit (e.g. via TLS) and at rest, applying
role-based access control so that users and staff can only access what their role
requires, and maintaining audit logs for security-relevant actions (logins,
permission changes, data exports, administrative actions).

Systems should also follow the principle of least privilege - granting the
minimum level of access needed for a user or component to perform its function -
and should have a plan for how security incidents (breaches, unauthorized
access attempts) are detected and responded to, rather than assuming they will
not occur.

Availability and reliability expectations, while not always a formal "compliance"
requirement, are commonly treated as part of a system's duty of care to its
users - especially for systems where downtime could cause real harm (financial
loss, safety risk, service denial). A reasonable baseline is documented uptime
targets, monitoring, and a defined incident-response process.

Any system - regardless of domain - that lacks authentication, uses unencrypted
storage or transmission of user data, or has no access logging would generally be
considered to fall below a reasonable security and privacy baseline.
